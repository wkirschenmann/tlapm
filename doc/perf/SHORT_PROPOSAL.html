01

What breaks, and where

tlapm is fine on small proofs and unusable on large ones, and the boundary is not gradual. The four specifications below are the same tool on the same machine: seventy obligations finish before you notice, and ten thousand do not finish at all.

specificationobligationsprepare, mainprepare, after
a small module71281 ms273 ms ×1.03
a 1 800-obligation synthetic module1 80047.2 s6.1 s ×7.74
a private refinement chain9 967timed out145 s
a private 30k-line monolith29 965—299 s

The failure is not slowness. On the monolith main has not been measured on this campaign, and on the refinement chain it is still preparing when our fifteen-minute ceiling stops it — a protocol timeout, so how much longer it would need is not known. The wall itself is not in doubt: on the private refinement chain and private 30k monolith the cap refuses an allocation outright at the commits marked with a cross in §5, and there is no number there to make faster.

The same wall stands in the editor. Re-checking the refinement chain after a single edit, with every fingerprint already in the cache, does not finish inside the fifteen-minute ceiling on main and takes 32.9 s after this series.

And at the keystroke: from didChange to publishDiagnostics, 44.7 s on main against 4.7 s after — ×9.5.

The constraint that shapes everything below: small proofs must not get slower. Every chart carries a 71-obligation module for exactly that reason — it is the control, not a result.
02

One mechanism, four consequences

One mechanism explains all of it. tlapm generates one obligation per proof leaf, and each obligation carries its whole context: the module scope, every definition in scope, and the statement of every theorem proved before it. The context therefore grows with the file, and the per-obligation work is a function of the context. Work per obligation grows with file size, and total work grows faster than the number of obligations.

That single fact has four distinct consequences, and this series has one family of change for each:

Repeated passes over the context

Expanding k definitions walked the context k times; detecting ENABLED axioms compared every axiom against every hypothesis; every hypothesis lookup walked a deque. Each is a factor of context size that need not be there.

PR2, PR3, PR7

Contexts kept alive

Every obligation of the run was materialised before the first prover started, and a process-lifetime memo table pinned one context per obligation prepared. Peak memory grew with the file, so a big enough file cannot run at any speed.

PR4

Context that no longer matters

After expansion, the hidden definitions and the instantiated statements of earlier theorems are unreachable from the goal — and are most of the weight shipped to the prover. Dropping them sends strictly less than today.

PR5

Context recomputed from scratch

Consecutive obligations share almost all of their context — on a 30k module, 699 of 743 hypotheses are the physically same objects as in the previous one — and all three preparation passes recomputed the whole thing.

PR6

Two changes sit outside that mechanism. One is the editor's proof-step tree, which scanned the obligation map once per step and had nothing to do with contexts. The other is the set of correctness fixes, which are here because without them the measurements that justify the rest are not available.

The correctness gate every commit passes

Not a benchmark gate — a soundness one. For each of the seventeen commits, in sequence: dune runtest src and dune runtest lsp green, and every one of the 48 test/fast tests passing with the full prover stack — Z3 4.8.9, Zenon, and Isabelle 2025 with the TLA+ heap built from this repository's isabelle/ sources. main is 48 of 48 under the same conditions, so the fail-set is empty on both sides. The gate is fail-set identity, not a pass count: a newly failing test is a regression even where the count would still look healthy.

Two conditions matter and are easy to get wrong. Putting isabelle on PATH is not enough — tlapm invokes it with a session root under its own backends directory, and without that link seven tests fail in a way that reads like a proof failure rather than a missing backend. And the tree must be clean of .tlacache: a fingerprint recorded by an earlier run with a different prover set replays as a failure, which is exactly how an earlier pass here reported 47 of 48 and blamed Z3.

Two invariants hold across the whole series. The provers receive a subset of what they receive today, never more, and no obligation is created that does not exist today. Fingerprints do not move: the digest is computed on the const-annotated pre-expansion obligation, and every change that removes context runs after that point, on the backend path only — so --printallobs output and cache hits are unchanged by construction rather than by testing.

What can be switched, and what cannot

Three of the seventeen commits carry a switch; the other 14 do not, and deliberately so — they are output-preserving changes to a single code path, so a flag would mean carrying two implementations of the same function and testing neither of them properly. Where a switch does exist it restores the original code rather than disabling a feature, which is what makes it usable as a differential reference.

Every measurement in this document is taken with the features on and the oracle off, which is the default state on both counts: the campaign invokes tlapm --noproving --nofp --cache-dir … with no --debug argument and with no TLAPM_* variable in the environment. Checked against the running processes, not assumed — the point of a switch that restores the original code is that forgetting to unset it would silently measure the wrong build.

The oracle is the one that must never become a default. It works by doing the job twice and comparing, so turning it on doubles the cost of the pass it checks; that is not a defect to optimise away but the mechanism itself. It is opt-in through the environment, and with the variable unset the added code is a single branch that is not taken.

One pair calibrates the noise better than the baseline repeat does. The editor obligation pool changes only files under lsp/, so the command-line preparation path across it cannot differ — whatever the campaign measures there is the run-to-run spread, on a pair whose true answer is known to be zero. It measures: small 1.4 % (memory 0.11 %), 600 1.8 % (memory 0.26 %), 1 800 0.2 % (memory 0.48 %), chain 0.4 % (memory 3.50 %), monolith 0.3 % (memory 0.00 %). That is the floor any ratio in §6 has to clear, and it is why a commit is only credited with an effect when it is a sustained step rather than a single large ratio. Read it per corpus: the floor is widest where the run is shortest, which is one reason the ratios in §6 are quoted on the 1 800-obligation corpus and the two private specifications rather than on the small ones.

The two private corpora are measured at the pull-request endpoints rather than at every commit. That is a deliberate bound, not an omission: a single preparation pass on the monolith is minutes when it completes and a quarter of an hour when it does not, and the question those corpora answer — does this pull request make the specification runnable — is a property of the pull request. Where a commit inside a pull request needed separating from its neighbour, the public 1 800-obligation corpus is measured at every commit and answers it.

Which stopped runs got a longer clock

The fifteen-minute ceiling earns its keep in exactly one place: the first pass over a line whose shape is unknown, where it stops one hanging point from consuming the campaign. Everywhere else it is fifteen minutes thrown away, and this campaign paid that bill before noticing — one monolith point already had its ceiling reading and was re-run at the same ceiling before being escalated, so it cost 900 s plus an hour to learn what the hour alone would have said. The clock is therefore chosen from what the data already holds: a point already stopped at the ceiling starts at the hour, and so does every point below it on the same line, since a slower commit cannot be a quicker one. Only a point with no history pays the cheap pass.

Which stopped runs are worth an hour at all is a separate question, and a run already stopped is not yet a fact about the commit, so some are re-run and some are not: an hour spent confirming a failure we already know is an hour not spent measuring something. The rule is two questions asked of the stopped run, and it runs only if either answer lands inside the budget. When would it finish? — taken from the ratio between this commit and the same commit on the public 1 800-obligation corpus, which does complete. When would it abort? — taken from extrapolating its own memory growth to the 12 GB cap. If the sooner of the two is past 90 % of the budget, the run is not launched and the point stays a ring.

The rule is an estimate and it is worth saying how badly it estimates, so here is its record on every point where it said yes and the answer came back.

05

Commit by commit

One point per commit, main at the left, in the order the series is proposed in. A red mark instead of a point means the run did not complete, and its shape says whether that is a result. A cross is a result: the run was refused memory, or it was given a full hour and still did not finish. A ring is a protocol timeout — inconclusive: the run was stopped by the ceiling this measurement protocol sets, not by anything in the commit, so it says where we stopped looking and nothing about where the commit ends up. It is not a slower version of a cross; it is the absence of an answer, and the count of outstanding rings is stated rather than left to be inferred. The tables in §6 say which of the two ways a real failure failed, because the difference matters — a change that speeds preparation up reaches the memory wall sooner, turning a ceiling into an abort without being a regression. Public and private corpora share each chart: hue separates them, dash separates sizes.

Commit labels are coloured by provenance: violet is a commit whose message credits tlaplus/tlapm#286, green is new here. Bold labels are the last commit of a pull request — the point a reviewer merging that pull request would land on.

The first chart is throughput rather than speedup for one reason: on the two private specifications main has no value to form a ratio against.

PR5

thr

2 commits · 1 file · 45c62cc 406e50d

An obligation ships its whole context to the prover, including hidden definitions and hidden facts that nothing in it can refer to. Dropping them sends the prover strictly less, and is what makes the largest specification complete.

src/backend/prep.ml +75 −0

07

Relation to issue #286

Six of these seventeen commits credit tlaplus/tlapm#286, and the issue itself describes four families of optimisation. The counts differ for a reason worth stating plainly, because it looks like inflation and is not.

The reference patchset put seven independent micro-optimisations in a single commit. Splitting that batch one subject per commit is the whole point of the exercise — each becomes reviewable on its own, and each becomes measurable on its own. Measuring them separately is what showed that five of the seven move nothing, so they are in §7 rather than here. Two survive: the deque lookups and the scheduler reaper. Add the single-pass expansion, the two prunes, and the linear ENABLED scan, and that is the six.

The other eleven commits are new. Three are the timing defects, two more are scheduler fixes, two are the memory pull request, three are the prefix-resume caches, one is the editor's obligation pool and one memoizes the grammar — and the pool is in a component the issue does not touch at all.

08

What is deliberately not here

Nine further commits were written, measured, and are not proposed. Each is correct and each removes real work; none of them moves a metric a user can observe. They are listed because the reason they are absent is a result, and because a reviewer asking “why not also…” deserves the answer rather than silence.

The bar is a sustained step, not a large single ratio, and applying it changed a decision. A tenth commit — memoizing the two instances of each grammar rule — was on this list because the editor-latency harness could not resolve it at ten repetitions. The lesson is in the method rather than in the commit: one metric failing to see a change is not the same as the change not being there.

What is still open after this series is a different kind of change rather than a smaller one, which is why none of it is here: the editor re-elaborates the whole document on every interaction and then a child process repeats the work; there is no in-process cache of elaborated EXTENDS dependencies; and obligations are generated eagerly for the whole file even when the request concerns one proof. Each of those is an architectural change with a design discussion in front of it, not a commit.
